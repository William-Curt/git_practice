Langmuir Probe Basics

A Langmuir probe is a small metal tip pushed into a plasma. You sweep its voltage (the bias) and record the current it collects. The shape of that current-versus-voltage curve tells you how hot the electrons are and how many there are. Below is your log from : sweeps from −10 V to +10 V and back. How to read this curve

1

First, remove what the electronics add

The current channel reads about 150 nA even with no plasma, plus a tiny leak that grows with voltage. The last seven sweeps of the log were taken with the plasma off, so we fit a straight line to them and subtract it from every sweep. Without this step the curve never crosses zero and the numbers below come out wrong.

2

Ion side (very negative bias)

At −10 V the probe repels electrons and only the heavier, slower ions reach it, so the current is small and negative: the ion saturation current. It is only a few tens of nanoamps here, and it barely changes with voltage.

3

Floating potential Vf

As the bias rises, the fastest electrons start to arrive. Where the electron and ion currents cancel, the net current is zero: that voltage is the floating potential, the voltage an insulated object in this plasma would settle at.

4

Transition region → electron temperature

Just above Vf the electron current grows exponentially with voltage, because the electrons have a spread of energies (a Maxwellian distribution). The steeper the rise, the colder the electrons. Fitting a straight line to ln(Ie) against V gives the electron temperature in electron-volts:

Te [eV] = 1 / slope of ln(Ie) vs V  ·  1 eV ≈ 11,600 K

In this log the rise is not a single clean exponential (the slope keeps changing), so Te depends a little on which part you fit. The dashed line shows the part used here.

5

Electron side and the plasma potential

Eventually the probe collects every electron that reaches it and the curve should bend over (the knee) at the plasma potential Vp, then flatten: the electron saturation current. In this log the current is still climbing at +10 V, so the knee lies beyond the sweep range. That is why the density below is a lower bound: it uses the current at +10 V instead of the true saturation current.

ne = Ies / ( e · A · √( e Te / 2π me ) )  ·  A = probe area (10 mm² assumed)
